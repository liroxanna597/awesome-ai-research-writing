\section{Results and Analysis}

\subsection{Multi-Case Sentiment Validation Results (RQ1)}

Across the three cases, the framework exhibits stable performance in temporal alignment while showing event-dependent variation in overall sentiment bias. In the Guangzhou BMW case, the complete model achieves \(\Delta\mathrm{Bias}=0.107\), and the 7-day setting further improves it to 0.064, indicating strong agreement with real sentiment levels. In the Cathay Pacific case, the complete model yields a moderate \(\Delta\mathrm{Bias}=0.291\), while the 300-agent setting improves the bias to 0.206. In contrast, the MU5735 case presents a much larger bias, with the complete model reaching \(\Delta\mathrm{Bias}=0.754\). This pattern suggests that extremely tragic events are harder to reproduce in terms of sentiment intensity, even when the direction of simulated sentiment is correct.

Despite these differences in level bias, \(\Delta\mathrm{Div}\) remains low across all cases and settings, and \(\mathrm{DTW}_{\mathrm{norm}}\) also stays within a relatively stable range. These results indicate that the framework consistently captures hourly fluctuation stability and coarse temporal shape, even when exact sentiment magnitude is difficult to match. By contrast, Pearson correlation does not reach strong levels in the three cases, suggesting that fine-grained short-term fluctuations remain difficult to reproduce under the current framework, likely because recommendation algorithms and hot-search mechanisms are not explicitly modeled.

\subsection{Cross-Case Comparison and Scale Effects (RQ1)}

Cross-case comparison reveals a clear gradient in difficulty: Guangzhou BMW shows the smallest bias, Cathay Pacific is intermediate, and MU5735 is the most difficult. This gradient closely matches the extremity of real-world emotional intensity. At the same time, increasing the number of agents from 100 to 300 improves \(\Delta\mathrm{Bias}\) in all three cases, with improvements of 33\% for Guangzhou BMW, 29\% for Cathay Pacific, and 9\% for MU5735. These results suggest that the framework is scalable, while also indicating that relatively small-scale simulations can already recover major statistical characteristics of group sentiment distributions.

Time-scale effects are case-dependent. In Guangzhou BMW and MU5735, the 7-day simulation improves \(\Delta\mathrm{Bias}\) compared with the 3-day simulation, implying that longer interaction and reflection windows can generate cumulative cognitive adjustment benefits. In Cathay Pacific, however, the 7-day simulation performs worse than the 3-day setting, probably because the real public discussion decays quickly after the first three days, making a longer simulated tail inconsistent with the real temporal pattern.

\subsection{Ablation Analysis (RQ2)}

The ablation results show strong case heterogeneity. In the Guangzhou BMW case, removing reflection worsens \(\Delta\mathrm{Bias}\) from 0.107 to 0.582, a 5.4-fold degradation, indicating that reflection is crucial for maintaining sentiment fidelity. Without reflection, psychological variables such as emotion intensity and social confidence remain nearly fixed, causing generated sentiment to drift toward neutrality and away from the strongly negative real data.

In MU5735 and Cathay Pacific, however, removing reflection reduces the observed bias. This does not mean that reflection is unnecessary; rather, it suggests that reflection may amplify negative emotion beyond the level that the current agent design can calibrate correctly. In other words, the mechanism pushes sentiment in the right direction, but in extreme or mixed-emotion cases, the calibrated magnitude may deviate further from real-world sentiment.

The ablation of official publishers also produces case-dependent effects. In Guangzhou BMW and Cathay Pacific, removing official publishers worsens \(\Delta\mathrm{Bias}\), implying that authoritative releases provide a positive anchoring effect that brings simulated sentiment closer to reality. MU5735 shows the opposite pattern: removing official publishers lowers the bias. We interpret this as evidence that official releases in extreme tragedies act as strong emotional catalysts. They intensify negative sentiment in the correct direction, but the simulated agents still fail to reach the true extreme emotional level of real data, so the stronger catalyst effect paradoxically increases level bias.

Taken together, the ablation results reveal three important findings. First, the Guangzhou BMW case most clearly demonstrates the intended effect of the framework: both reflection and official publishers contribute independently to simulation fidelity. Second, the MU5735 case exposes an emotional-ceiling effect in extremely tragic scenarios. Third, the Cathay Pacific case suggests a mixed-effect regime in which official publishers behave as expected, while reflection may require finer calibration in relatively moderate events.

\subsection{Responses to Official Publisher Events (RQ3)}

The role of official releases also differs across the three cases. In the Guangzhou BMW case, the first official police report functions as an emotional catalyst, sharply increasing attention and pushing sentiment into a strongly negative regime. Later judicial updates provide a temporary emotional relief effect, but are followed by renewed negative activation as agents reflect more deeply on the meaning of the event. In the MU5735 case, official releases mainly generate sustained emotional deepening rather than temporary regulation. Agents repeatedly internalize grief after receiving new updates, producing a loop of \emph{release \(\rightarrow\) deepening \(\rightarrow\) stabilization \(\rightarrow\) renewed deepening}. In the Cathay Pacific case, company apologies and follow-up measures generate a weaker emotional-relief effect, suggesting that corporate responses enjoy lower trust than institutional or judicial actions.

A cross-case comparison indicates two relevant dimensions. The first is information type: factual releases mainly function as emotional catalysts, whereas accountability-oriented releases act more like emotional regulators. The second is event severity: the more severe the event, the harder it is for institutional responses to produce visible emotional relief. This pattern is broadly consistent with crisis-communication expectations that event severity moderates the effectiveness of official intervention.
